## PART D — Consistency of the paper's results with the literature

**(i) CHM15k Rayleigh-calibrated β_att within +0.3 to +4.3 % of EARLINET (Leipzig, Măgurele).** Published CHM15k-vs-Raman-lidar comparisons: Heese et al. 2010 (Leipzig, CHM15k vs PollyXT): structures agree but "The calibration of the ceilometer profiles by an independent measurement of the aerosol optical depth (AOD) by a sun photometer is necessary to determine the correct magnitude" — no percent agreement; Madonna et al. 2015 (Potenza, CHM15k vs MUSA): R = 0.95 but the CHM15k was *normalised to MUSA* and its constant varied by 58 % over 6 months (vs 19 % for MUSA), and the CHM15k "is overestimated" at high backscatter values (Sect. 4); Wiegner et al. 2014: CHM15kx vs CL51 vs EARLINET at Munich/Potenza, qualitative; Bellini et al. 2024: no β_att-vs-lidar comparison (AOD vs photometer, 70–84 % within ±(0.01 + 0.15·AOD)); Wandinger et al. 2016: EARLINET reference systems agree within ±2 % at signal level (355/532 nm only). So the manuscript's +0.3/+4.3 % medians are **tighter than anything published for a CHM15k**, which is plausible but should be framed carefully: both the EARLINET 1064 nm elastic product and the ceilometer are molecular-normalised in the same height range, so the comparison partly tests the consistency of two Rayleigh normalisations and the (unreferenced) 10–20 % uncertainty of the 1064 nm elastic reference bounds what can be claimed. The Payerne result (+30 % vs CL61, attributed to dark) shows the EARLINET agreement is unit-specific — say so explicitly in the Conclusions, which currently quote only the 0.3–4.3 %.

**(ii) 30–40 % peak-to-peak seasonal cycle of the CHM15k Rayleigh constant attributed to instrument temperature.** Literature amplitudes: Buxmann 2024 — "The fluctuations typically range up to a factor of 3 of the minimum calibration constants" and product "uncertainties of up to 30-60 %"; Van Hove & Diémoz 2024 — amplitudes at 70 E-PROFILE stations "do not reveal any clear geographical pattern", with "significant similarities … between the variation of the calibration factor and … the laser pulse numbers, the detector sensitivity and the background noise"; Madonna 2015 — 58 % variability, r = 0.6 with ambient temperature; Madonna 2018 — laser pulse count ±10 % seasonal; Bellini 2024 — cycle present, origin "under investigation", removed by filtering. The paper's 30–40 % is thus at the low end of reported amplitudes, and its housekeeping-parameter argument is literally Van Hove & Diémoz's finding (must be cited). Two published facts conflict with a pure "instrument sensitivity" reading: (a) Buxmann 2024 shows synthetic residual aerosol in the calibration window changes C_L by ×1.2–2 and finds residual aerosol with a Raymetrics lidar on E-PROFILE calibration days ("a combination of both internal instrument related parameters as well as atmospheric conditions contribute"); (b) Hopkin et al. 2019 obtained a CHM15k *cloud* calibration with "no sign of any annual cycle" (90-d running mean 0.48 ± 0.02, SD 4 % over 2015) — a genuine sensitivity change should appear in both methods. The paper's own App. 6 offers a reconciliation it does not draw: the hood dark lowers the Rayleigh constant by ~20 % and Le et al. 2026 show hood backgrounds vary with internal temperature, so a temperature-dependent *baseline* (invisible to the cloud method, visible to the Rayleigh fit at 3–6 km) would produce exactly a Rayleigh-only seasonal cycle correlated with housekeeping temperatures. Recommend rewording 5.1.3 to "consistent with an instrumental origin (temperature-dependent sensitivity and/or baseline; Madonna 2015, 2018; Van Hove & Diémoz 2024), although residual aerosol can contribute (Buxmann 2024)".

**(iii) CL61 Rayleigh/cloud ratio median 0.94 and the dark-signal explanation.** Consistent with Le et al. 2026 (four sites: the two methods "agree well within their respective uncertainties", cloud factor uncertainty ~10 %) and with Looschelders 2025 (six co-located CL61s: calibration coefficients within ~5 %; hood dark profiles small and "stable over different time subsets"; O'Connor et al. 2024: stable, slight T dependence 15–31 °C) and Filioglou 2023 (factory calibration within 15 %). The paper's dark of −7 to −28 % of the molecular signal at 3–6 km at Payerne is larger than anything these works report, but they did not quantify the dark relative to the molecular return at that range; Le et al. 2026 provide the remedy (temperature-indexed hood LUT subtraction) and should be cited for it in App. 6 ("Methods to estimate the dark remotely are under development"). Laffineur et al. 2026 report the Uccle CL61 factory cloud calibration "remained stable" — consistent with the paper's statement that the cloud constant is dark-immune, and worth citing since the Uccle unit is singled out.

**(iv) CL51 Rayleigh constant ×1.38 above the cloud constant.** No published CL51 Rayleigh-vs-cloud ratio exists; the sign is consistent with Wiegner et al. 2019 (range-corrected CL51 signal "increase … with height in the free troposphere", hood artefacts 3–8 km → a Rayleigh fit sees too much signal → C_L too high), with Geifl 2026 (temperature-dependent range distortion; unit- and temperature-dependence explains 0.99 at Keflavík vs 1.2–2.5 elsewhere), with Jin 2015 (factory constants unreliable) and Chen 2025 (background correction mandatory at 910 nm). Day-to-day scatter 20 % vs 10 % is consistent with Wiegner 2014's "low SNR might introduce quite high statistical uncertainties".

**(v) CHM15k cloud constants 32–42 % below the Rayleigh constants because of saturation.** Sign agrees with Hopkin 2019 ("the backscatter reported for this profile is false – it is too low", so B too low and C too high, i.e. C_L too low). Magnitude: not published — Hopkin never compared cloud and Rayleigh constants; their Fig. 13 gives relative cloud calibrations "less than 1.0" for most Met Office CHM15k. The paper acknowledges that its screening "contains no such test"; the 32–42 % is therefore an upper bound obtained with saturated profiles and clouds below 1 km included, not a property of the method. To be fair to Hopkin, the conclusion "the cloud method is therefore not applied to the photon-counting instruments" should be qualified: with Hopkin's negative-overshoot test and a >1–2.2 km base limit the method gave a 4 %-stable CHM15k calibration. Note also the Rayleigh day-to-day scatter quoted here (15–25 %) is larger than Hopkin's cloud-calibration SD (4 % of 90-d means; daily scatter larger) — comparable statistics (daily vs 90-d) should be used.

**(vi) Multiple-scattering factors of Table A4-1 (CL31 0.89/0.84/0.76 at 0.5/1/2 km; CL51/CL61 0.92/0.87/0.80; CHM15k 0.97/0.95/0.91).** O'Connor 2004 (CT75K, FOV 0.66–0.75 mrad, D_eff 8–20 µm, α = 15–20 km⁻¹, Eloranta model): η = 0.83 ± 0.09 at 1 km, 0.79 at 1.5 km, 0.74 ± 0.04 at 2.7 km, 0.73 ± 0.06 at 4 km. The CL31 values (FOV 0.83 mrad) 0.84 at 1 km and 0.76 at 2 km fall inside O'Connor's envelope — consistent. Hopkin 2019: "η is usually between 0.7 and 0.85 for wavelengths between 905 and 1064 nm" — the paper's CHM15k/Mini-MPL values (0.91–0.97) lie *above* that range; defensible for 0.11–0.23 mrad FOV but should be commented on, because the operational choice of r_e = 5.5 µm (D_eff = 11 µm, at the small end of O'Connor's 8–20 µm) and α = 10 km⁻¹ (below O'Connor's 15–20 km⁻¹) both push η towards unity; O'Connor's stated ±10 % calibration uncertainty from the η range should be propagated and quoted. The paper's Fig. 4e ("theoretical ηS curve computed from the droplet effective radius measured by Cloudnet that day") implies a day-specific r_e — but App. 4 says a climatological 5.5 µm; make the two consistent.

**(vii) Water-vapour correction magnitude "15–20 %" at 910 nm.** For broad-line Vaisala spectra (CL31 ≈ 6 nm, CL51 ≈ 3.4 nm): Hopkin 2019 two-way transmission 0.75–0.85 for IWV 2–1 cm (annual cycle of the constant ≈ 12 % if uncorrected); Wiegner & Gasteiger 2015: ~20 % (mid-latitudes) to >50 % (tropics) in β_p; Wiegner 2019: correction validated for CL51. For the CL61 narrow line (910.55 ± 0.08 nm): Le et al.'s reply to RC1 finds the two-way WV attenuation ~10× smaller than for the CL51 ("minimal"), and 910.55 nm is the DA10 DIAL offline wavelength. The manuscript applies the 15–20 % statement to "the 910 nm instruments (the CL61)" — **inconsistent with the literature it relies on and with its own App. 1** (the broad-line model over-corrected the CL61 and produced +9 % km⁻¹). Replace by the actual LUT numbers per instrument (CL31/CL51 ≈ 15–25 %; CL61 ≈ a few %).

**(viii) S_liq = 18.8 sr and its droplet-size/wavelength dependence.** O'Connor 2004: 18.8 ± 0.8 sr at 905 nm for median diameters ~10–50 µm (γ = 2–10); 18.6 ± 1 sr at 532 nm and 18.9 ± 0.4 sr at 355 nm; drizzle (>50 µm) lowers S. Hopkin 2019 quotes Pinnick et al. 1983 (18.2 sr) and Wu et al. 2011 (18.5 ± 0.47 sr) at 1064 nm and uses 18.8 sr for both 905 and 1064 nm. Using 18.8 sr for the 532 nm Mini-MPL and 1064 nm CHM15k is therefore within ~3 %; say so, and keep the 500 m lower CBH limit and drizzle screening because S departs from 18.8 sr only for drizzle-sized drops.

**(ix) CBH difference Vaisala vs Lufft "up to 300 m".** Published: Martucci 2010 — manufacturer products R² = 0.788, mean intercept 160 m, worst case 569 m (CL31 vs CHM15K, 2008 firmware); Wagner et al. 2024 — "approx. 50–100 m difference for liquid water clouds" (Vaisala above the signal maximum, Lufft/Campbell near cloud bottom, MiniMPL ≈ 100 m lower); Klaus, Görsdorf et al. 2026 — differences dominated by firmware algorithms rather than by the backscatter data. No located source gives 300 m; write "typically 50–100 m, occasionally several hundred metres (Martucci et al. 2010; Wagner et al. 2024)".

## Summary of main problems

1. **Phantom and mis-assigned references**: Hogan 2006 entry does not exist (use Appl. Opt. 45, 5984 + JAS 2008); Klett 1983 is not the inversion paper; Freudenthaler 2018 was never published; Stevens 2019 DOI points to another paper; Görsdorf 2016 "pp. 27-30" are conference dates and the paper cannot be found; An 2019 and Münkel & Roininen 2010 incomplete.
2. **10 cited works with no entry** (D'Amico, Engelmann, Freudenthaler, Frisch, Madonna 2015, Miles, Nicolae ×2, Wandinger, US Std. Atm.) and **9 data/standards sources cited by name only** (HITRAN, CAMS, Cloudnet, CL61 guide, WMO-No. 8, ICAO Annex 3, WMO-No. 306, ISO 28902-4, raw2l1/A-Profiles); **14 entries never cited**, three of which (Buxmann 2024, Van Hove & Diémoz 2024, Illingworth 2019) are essential to the paper's own arguments.
3. **Mis-attributions**: the 0.188 nm CL61 line width is not in Le et al. 2026 (only in their RC1 reply, as an unpublished personal communication); Bellini 2024 does not say the CHM15k constant is temperature-dependent (Madonna 2015/2018 and Van Hove & Diémoz do); Wagner 2024 gives 50–100 m, not 300 m, CBH differences; the CL31 is not cloud-calibrated by the manufacturer (the CL61 is); the CL61 is an analogue APD system, not photon-counting; Frisch 2002 is a radar retrieval, not in-situ data; Spänkuch's 0.03/0.05 are human-observer thresholds.
4. **Internal inconsistency on water vapour**: "15–20 %" bias is claimed for the CL61, whose narrow 910.55 nm line (per the paper's own App. 1 and Le et al.'s reply) has ~10× less absorption than the CL51.
5. **Framing of 2010**: Flentje 2010 and Emeis 2011 show a national operational ceilometer network did track the ash plume.
6. **Seasonal cycle**: the temperature attribution ignores Buxmann's residual-aerosol evidence and Hopkin's flat CHM15k cloud calibration; the paper's own dark-signal results suggest a temperature-dependent baseline as the mechanism.
7. **Top missing papers**: Illingworth 2015; Cimini 2020; Flentje 2010/Emeis 2011; Heese 2010; Hogan 2006/2008 + Eloranta 1998; Madonna 2018; Roininen 2025 SOP CL61; Muñiz-Rosado 2026; Speidel & Vogelmann 2023; Kar 2018; Gordon 2022/Peuch 2022/Illingworth 2007; Klaus et al. 2026; Sauvageat 2026 dataset; Laffineur 2026.
